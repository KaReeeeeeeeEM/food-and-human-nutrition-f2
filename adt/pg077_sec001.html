Food and Human Nutrition

Form Two

Care should be taken to ensure that proper breastfeeding is practised, and complementary foods are prepared under good hygienic conditions to avoid contamination. Meals for children should be balanced and proper feeding practices such as feeding frequency should be observed. This will ensure adequate food intake and will prevent the risk of malnourishment.

Proper nutrition from conception to two years of the child’s life also known as 1000 days ensures the best possible start in life with long-term benefits.

Children with prolonged states of malnourishment can develop different health and physical problems which may delay their physical growth, mental development and lower Intelligent

Quotient (IQ) as well as social skills.

The child will also be vulnerable to diseases.

Adolescents: This is a critical stage of human lifecycle whereby individuals are

undergoing

anatomical

and

physiological maturational changes in the preparation for adulthood.

Adolescents are persons from 10 to

19 years old who are experiencing biological,

psychological,

mental

and social changes. During this period, they have higher nutritional requirements than during pre-puberty and adulthood. Nutritional deficiencies and poor eating habits established during this period can hinder attaining

normal adult height, delay sexual and reproductive maturation and lead to micronutrient

deficiency

disorders.

Nutrition of adolescent girls is quite essential because they are likely to take reproductive roles any time at this stage of their growth.

Women of Reproductive Age (WRA):

These are women aged between 15 and 49 years. Ensuring good nutrition is important for their health as they are likely to take reproductive roles any time. WRA are at risk of being anaemic if they receive inadequate foods, lack micronutrients such as iron, folic acid, vitamin C and vitamin B12. These are necessary nutrients for blood formation.

Pregnant women: A healthier pregnancy depends on what the mother is consuming. A pregnant woman and the foetus should be supplied with sufficient nutrients. Poor nutrient intake can subject the mother and her unborn baby to the risk of malnourishment. Iron, folic acid, vitamin B12, calcium, phosphorus, energy and protein foods are necessary for the pregnant woman. Inadequate intake of iron can lead to anaemia which may result in birth complications such as still birth, abortion and low birth weight. Folic acid is essential just before and during pregnancy to ensure proper closure of the neural tube. It is important because it can prevent birth defects of the brain and spinal cord.

These birth defects are called Neural

Tube Defects (NTDs).
